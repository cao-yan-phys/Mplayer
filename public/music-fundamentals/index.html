Tonal music fundamentals

Explore pitch, rhythm, scales and harmony through short examples.

01 · Pitch

Pitch describes how high or low a sound is. The higher note of an octave has twice the frequency of the lower note. In twelve-tone equal temperament, an octave is divided into twelve equal semitone steps. A whole tone spans two semitones.

C4 · 261.63 Hz

On the staff, successive lines and spaces follow the note names A–G. A clef assigns pitches to these positions. ♯ raises a note by a semitone; ♭ lowers it; ♮ restores the unaltered note. C♯ and D♭ share a key in equal temperament, but their spelling reflects different musical relationships.

Reference pitch sets the frequency of a chosen note. ISO 16 specifies A4 = 440 Hz. Historical pitch varied between places, periods and instruments. Many Baroque ensembles today use A4 = 415 Hz, approximately one semitone below 440 Hz. Surviving tuning forks also give values near 412 and 423 Hz; see Jeremy Montagu’s Why Differing Pitch Standards?

Reference pitch fixes the overall pitch level. Temperament determines how intervals are tuned within the octave.

02 · Rhythm

Note values describe relative durations. A half note lasts twice as long as a quarter note; an eighth note lasts half as long. A dot extends a note or rest by half its original value. A rest marks silence; a tie joins notes of the same pitch into one sustained sound.

In 4/4 there are four quarter-note beats per bar. In 3/4 there are three. In 6/8, six eighth notes usually form two larger beats, each divided into three. Tempo specifies the rate of the beat. Here, BPM counts quarter-note beats in 4/4 and 3/4, and dotted-quarter beats in 6/8.

03 · Intervals

An interval is the distance between two pitches. Its number counts note names, including both endpoints; its quality specifies the size more precisely. C–E is a major third; C–E♭ is a minor third.

A melodic interval is heard in sequence; a harmonic interval is heard simultaneously. Consonant intervals tend to sound stable; dissonant intervals create tension whose resolution depends on the surrounding harmony.

04 · Scales & modes

A scale is an ordered collection of pitches, usually presented across an octave. The steps of a major scale are 2–2–1–2–2–2–1 semitones. For natural minor, they are 2–1–2–2–1–2–2. Scale degrees number the notes from the tonic, or first degree.

Compared with natural minor, harmonic minor raises the seventh degree. The classical melodic minor scale raises the sixth and seventh when ascending and returns to natural minor when descending. Dorian is natural minor with a raised sixth; Mixolydian is major with a lowered seventh.

05 · Keys & tonality

A key organises music around a tonic note and its chord, which provide a sense of tonal centre. A key signature specifies sharps or flats that apply by default; accidentals alter notes within a passage.

Relative major and minor share a key signature but have different tonics. Parallel major and minor share a tonic but use different scales. Transposition moves a passage by a constant interval; modulation establishes a new tonal centre within a piece.

06 · Chords & inversions

A chord combines three or more distinct pitches. A triad has three distinct notes that can be arranged as two stacked thirds above a root. Major, minor, diminished and augmented triads differ in the size of those thirds. A seventh chord adds one more third.

In root position, the root is the lowest note. First inversion puts the third in the bass; second inversion puts the fifth in the bass. A seventh chord also has a third inversion, with the seventh in the bass. An arpeggio sounds the notes of a chord in sequence.

07 · Harmony & cadences

Roman numerals identify a chord's root by scale degree; uppercase denotes major triads and lowercase denotes minor triads. In major, I is tonic, IV is subdominant and V is dominant. Tonic provides a point of rest; dominant creates an expectation of return to tonic.

A cadence closes a phrase through melody, bass, harmony and rhythm. Voice leading describes how each voice moves between chords. Stepwise motion and held common tones connect successive harmonies.

Non-chord tones are notes outside the accompanying chord. Passing notes connect chord tones by step. Neighbour notes move away by step and return. Suspensions sustain a note from a previous chord, creating a dissonance that resolves by step.
